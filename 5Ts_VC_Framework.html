I didn't set out to build a framework. I set out to not be the person in the room who asks a shallow question.

Scouting deals for a Bay Area VC firm as an undergraduate is a strange job. You sit across from founders who have been working on a problem for five years, and you have forty-five minutes. You learn quickly that the quality of your questions is the only real edge you have. The "5Ts" — TAM, Team, Technology, Traction, and Term Sheet — is the standard vocabulary of early-stage VC. Every analyst knows it. But I found that each T has a shadow: a second-order question that shows up when things go wrong, and that experienced investors have usually quietly internalized. This is my attempt to make those shadows explicit.

The case studies woven throughout draw on real investment memos from my time at AIX Ventures — including Daxe.AI, a seed-stage AI data security company, and Hopo Therapeutics, a biotech developing a first-of-its-kind heavy metal chelator — to show what each T actually looks like when evaluated in the field.

T1
TAM + Threats
Total Addressable Market — and who else is swimming in it

Total Addressable Market is the first question because it sets the ceiling. If a company executes perfectly and captures every customer in their market, how big does the prize get? At the seed stage, you're not betting on perfect execution — you're betting on whether perfection could even matter. A company that nails its product in a $40M market is a nice outcome. It is not a venture-scale outcome.

But TAM alone is a trap. I have sat in calls where a founder presents a $50 billion market, which is technically correct, and then describes a go-to-market strategy that could realistically reach 0.1% of it. The Serviceable Addressable Market (SAM) and Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM) are the honest numbers. The gap between TAM and SOM is where a lot of seed memos quietly bury risk.

This is where Threats enters. Every TAM has a competitive structure — fragmented or consolidated, cyclical or stable, fast-growing or plateauing. The Threats layer forces the question: who else is already here, and what happens if a larger player decides to care?

"The worst competitive analysis isn't one that gets the competition wrong. It's one that lists every competitor and then explains why none of them matter."

What to actually ask

  • What is the SAM, not just the TAM? Who exactly are the buyers, and how do they currently purchase?
  • Is the market fragmented (easier to enter, harder to dominate) or consolidated (harder to enter, winner-take-most)?
  • What would it take for Google, AWS, or the incumbent market leader to build this in 18 months? Is there a structural reason they won't?
  • Are there regulatory tailwinds or headwinds specific to this market that compress or extend the runway?
  • What does the competitive moat look like in year three, not year one?
Case Study Hopo Therapeutics — Heavy Metal Chelation Therapeutics

Hopo's TAM sits at roughly $7.3 billion, addressing over one billion people worldwide affected by heavy metal poisoning — a problem with deep roots and almost no good solutions. The market risk score on their evaluation was a 7.1/10, reflecting genuine scale and non-cyclical demand. Lead poisoning doesn't follow the S&P 500.

But the Threats analysis is where it gets nuanced. The market is fragmented — approximately six competitors, none at a comparable stage. That sounds clean, until you dig into the product: Hopo's drug must clear FDA approval before it can be sold commercially. The effective competitive risk isn't the current field; it's other drugs in pre-testing phases that could reach commercialization on a parallel track. Hopo's accelerated FDA pipeline (they skip certain phases due to the drug's profile) is the moat — but it is a time-sensitive one.

Case Study Daxe.AI — Confidential AI for Regulated Sectors

Daxe operates in the AI data security and retrieval space, targeting federal, legal, and financial services clients. The TAM framing here is a classic trap — the "AI for enterprise" market is enormous. But Daxe's real SAM is the much smaller segment of regulated sectors that cannot use commercial LLMs due to data sovereignty requirements: DoD-level clients, legal firms with iron-clad confidentiality obligations, government intelligence applications.

The Threats picture is genuinely crowded — direct competitors (Pinecone, Elastic, Glean), legal-specific players (Casetext, Alexi), and the ever-present shadow of Google, AWS, and Meta building their way into the space. The saving grace is structural: large players aren't specifically targeting federal agencies, and Daxe has an early contractual relationship with the DoD that most seed-stage companies can't claim. That's not an accident — it's the moat. The Threats analysis ultimately becomes an argument for why the moat, while narrow, is genuinely defensible if they establish traction before the incumbents notice the gap.

T2
Team + Talent
The founders you meet — and the team they'll build

Team evaluation is the section where every VC memo sounds the same: "strong domain expertise," "complementary skill sets," "serial entrepreneur." These phrases are not wrong, but they describe the founding team at a single point in time. The better question is about velocity: can this team build the organization they'll need to be in three years?

This is why Talent is the second T. Founding teams are usually excellent at one or two things and have significant blind spots in others. That's not a flaw — it's the nature of early-stage companies. The flaw is when founders either don't know their blind spots or don't prioritize closing them. The quality of the team they hire in months six through eighteen often tells you more about the company's ceiling than the founders' own resumes.

Green flags

Founders who can articulate specifically what roles they need to hire and why. Self-awareness about individual gaps. Prior relationships with high-quality operators who would join them.

Red flags

Believing the founding team can scale indefinitely without specialized hires. Founder dependency — where the company's core competency lives entirely in one person's head.

What to actually ask

  • What are the three most important hires in the next 12 months, and do the founders know how to recruit for those roles?
  • Where does the founding team have genuine domain depth, and where are they figuring it out as they go?
  • Is there key-person risk? What happens if the technical lead leaves?
  • How does the team make decisions — consensus, hierarchy, or domain ownership?
  • Can you talk to someone who has worked with the founders before, not just someone they've introduced you to?
Case Study Daxe.AI — Two-Person Founding Team

Daxe's founders are, individually, impressive. Erika (CEO) brings data analytics and pipeline management experience from Merkle, where she managed programs for Fortune 500 clients. Barak (CTO) built knowledge graphs at Google, founded the MapGraph project at Lyft, and holds two graph-based patents — his credentials in the exact technical space Daxe operates in are hard to replicate.

The Talent analysis, however, surfaces a real risk: Daxe is a two-person team carrying the full weight of a seed-stage startup in a technically complex, sales-intensive market. All technical depth sits with Barak. Erika's go-to-market background is in analytics, not enterprise software sales — which is what regulated-sector clients actually require. Our recommendation was clear: a COO, a CMO with government sales experience, and a small engineering team are not optional additions. They are preconditions for the company's next stage. The capital injection exists, in part, to unlock that hiring.

Case Study Hopo Therapeutics — Tight-Knit Three-Person Team

Hopo earned one of the highest management risk scores in our evaluation framework: 7.8/10. The founding trio — a serial biotech entrepreneur, a former LEK Life Sciences consultant, and a professor at Lawrence Berkeley — bring genuinely distinct skill sets that map directly to the company's three major challenges: science, business development, and academic credibility.

The Talent layer surfaces a different kind of question: the team of three, plus interns, is appropriate for the current clinical trial stage. It will not be appropriate for manufacturing scale-up. The company will need to add operational and regulatory expertise before FDA approval, not after. Proactively scoping that hiring plan — and ensuring founders are thinking about their Bakar Labs space alongside a future manufacturing facility — is the work of a good board, not a good investor presentation.

T3
Technology + Trademark
The product — and whether it can be protected

Technology evaluation at the seed stage is part technical diligence and part creative writing. You are evaluating a product that mostly doesn't exist yet, built by a team that will have to rebuild large parts of it before finding product-market fit, in a market that doesn't fully understand what it wants. Despite all of this, the technology question matters enormously — not because the current implementation is permanent, but because it reveals the depth of the founder's understanding of the problem space.

The Trademark layer — IP, patents, and defensibility — is where the long-term value of the technology either locks in or leaks out. A product that works brilliantly but can be replicated in six months by a well-funded competitor is a feature, not a company. The question isn't just "does this work?" It's "can this be owned?"

"The best early-stage technology isn't necessarily the most sophisticated. It's the most difficult to reproduce at the moment it begins to matter."

What to actually ask

  • Is the core technical insight novel, or is this a well-executed implementation of something widely known?
  • What IP exists today — patents filed, patents granted, trade secrets, proprietary datasets?
  • Are there dependency risks? Does the technology rely on a third-party platform or model that could alter terms or be acquired?
  • What is the technology's current limitation, and is the founding team honest about it?
  • Does the IP sit entirely with the company, or are there joint ownership arrangements (universities, labs, prior employers) that could complicate an exit?
Case Study Daxe.AI — Graph RAG in a Confidential Computing Environment

Daxe's core technology is a two-layer stack: a confidential computing environment that shields proprietary data from the underlying LLM's owners, and a graphical querying system that improves retrieval accuracy over standard RAG implementations. Neither layer is science fiction — both have technical precedents. But the combination, targeted specifically at regulated-sector compliance requirements, is where the differentiation lives.

The Trademark assessment is promising but not complete. Barak holds two graph-based patents, which provides some floor of defensibility. The deeper IP question is whether the confidential computing implementation can be separately protected, or whether it relies on open-source and third-party infrastructure in ways that reduce its proprietary surface area. Additionally, Daxe is currently text-only — image and multimedia support are on the roadmap. That limitation is a real competitive exposure in legaltech (where scanned documents are ubiquitous) and should be part of any honest IP roadmap conversation.

Case Study Hopo Therapeutics — A Hard-to-Reproduce Molecule

Hopo's technology risk score was one of the strongest we evaluated: 7.1/10, with a substitution risk score of 8.0 — meaning the drug is genuinely difficult to reproduce. HOPO is the first oral heavy metal chelator designed to target multiple heavy metals with minimal side effects, and the synthesis complexity provides a natural moat beyond the formal patent protection.

The Trademark nuance here is the LBNL royalty arrangement. The core molecule was developed at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, and the commercialization structure includes a royalty in the low single digits. That's not inherently a problem — but it introduces a dependency that a pure IP ownership structure wouldn't have. The key diligence question is: what happens to the royalty structure if LBNL's research priorities shift, or if a major partner acquires LBNL's interest in adjacent technology? The 30-year IP runway is substantial; the institutional dependency is the variable to watch.

T4
Traction + Timeline
Early signals — and how long until they compound

Traction is the section where investors get seduced by vanity metrics. Revenue growth rate before product-market fit. User counts without engagement. Letters of intent that don't convert. The discipline here is asking what the traction actually proves, not just what number looks impressive in a deck.

At the seed stage, the most valuable form of traction is often proof that the problem is real: design partners who integrate deeply, regulated clients who accept the security terms, a government agency that allows testing on live data. These signals are harder to fake and harder to find than an early growth curve on a freemium product.

The Timeline layer is the one most founders underestimate. Some markets require long sales cycles — government contracts, FDA approvals, enterprise procurement processes — that can stretch the distance between first customer conversation and first dollar by twelve to thirty-six months. That's not a reason not to invest. But it changes the capital requirements, the team's morale management, and the milestone structure needed to justify a Series A.

What to actually ask

  • What is the current traction, and what specifically does it prove about willingness to pay?
  • How long is the sales cycle for the primary customer segment, and what's the longest it has been in practice?
  • What is the current runway, and does it cover the time to a meaningful commercial milestone?
  • Are there regulatory, procurement, or approval processes (FDA, FedRAMP, SOC2) that add mandatory time to the go-to-market timeline?
  • If the product-market fit search takes 18 months longer than projected, what does the path forward look like?
Case Study Daxe.AI — A DoD Relationship at Pre-Seed

For a pre-seed company, Daxe's traction is unusually substantive. The DoD has allowed Daxe to train and test its technology on public sector data — a permission that large, well-resourced companies spend years earning, and that most seed-stage startups cannot point to at all. The active engagement with the USPTO adds a second data point. Together, they suggest that the federal sector problem Daxe is solving is recognized by the buyers with the most stringent evaluation criteria in the market.

The Timeline analysis is where the investment structure responds to the risk. Government procurement cycles are notoriously long — contract cycles of five years are standard, and losing a re-competition to a competitor like Pinecone could mean millions in lost revenue. Our investment recommendation was therefore sized accordingly: a small check now, with the explicit intention to double down at the seed round if Daxe hits customer signing targets in the next few quarters. The traction validates the problem. The timeline requires patience in the capital structure.

Case Study Hopo Therapeutics — The Biotech Timeline Problem

Hopo's traction is uniquely structured by the nature of biotech: Phase 1 clinical trials completed with clear safety data, an accelerated FDA path, and substantive interest from BARDA, NIH, and the IAEA. These are not soft signals. Government agencies don't engage with drugs they don't believe in. And the patient community's enthusiasm for expedited trials (people diagnosed with gadolinium poisoning actively wanting to be involved) provides a qualitative data point that is difficult to engineer.

But the Timeline is the central investment challenge. Hannah projected a 4-year ROI horizon, with $30M in annual government contract revenue following FDA approval. That's a credible model, given the trajectory. It also means this is a 4-year hold before the return materializes — with additional capital required for Phase 2 and Phase 3 trials along the way. The risk-adjusted return is strong; the liquidity timeline is long. An investor who needs a 2-year exit is in the wrong deal. An impact-oriented VC with a long duration fund is in the right one.

T5
Term Sheet + Thesis
The deal — and whether it fits the story you're telling

The term sheet is where a good deal either stays good or quietly becomes a bad one. Valuation, dilution, pro-rata rights, liquidation preferences, information rights — each term is a negotiation between the investor's need for downside protection and the founder's need to retain motivation and equity. Getting this wrong doesn't just hurt the current round. It poisons cap tables for every subsequent raise.

The Thesis layer is the one that separates a great investment memo from a check-the-box exercise. Every investment should have a thesis: a specific, falsifiable belief about why this company can win, why now is the right time, and why this team is the right one to do it. A thesis isn't a summary of what the company does. It's a bet about the future.

Thesis also encompasses portfolio fit: is this deal consistent with the fund's strategy, check size, and ownership targets? A great deal in the wrong fund is still a bad investment for both parties.

Terms to scrutinize

Liquidation preferences (1x non-participating is standard; multiples are founder-hostile). Pro-rata rights for future rounds. Information rights and board composition — especially at pre-seed where a controlling seat can distort the founding dynamic.

Building a thesis

A good thesis has three components: a market insight (why this problem is real), a timing insight (why now), and a team insight (why this specific group of people). Any investment thesis missing one of these three is incomplete.

What to actually ask

  • What is the post-money valuation, and is it consistent with the stage and comparable deals in this sector?
  • What does the cap table look like — and are there any prior investors, SAFE holders, or convertible notes that will dilute in unexpected ways?
  • Does the fund's thesis support this deal, and does this deal's check size fit within the fund's portfolio construction?
  • What are the specific milestones that would justify a follow-on investment at the next round?
  • What is the investment thesis in one sentence — not what the company does, but what you believe to be true about the future that justifies the bet?
Case Study Daxe.AI — A $1.5M Raise in a Competitive AI Market

Daxe is raising a $1.5M pre-seed round. Our recommendation was to participate with a small check, non-lead position — preserving the option to double down at the seed round if commercial milestones are hit. This is deliberate portfolio construction, not hedging: the risk in the current round is primarily execution risk (can they close government clients quickly?), which will resolve in the next two to three quarters. Taking a larger stake now, before that risk is resolved, concentrates exposure without changing the outcome.

The Thesis for Daxe can be stated directly: The recent AI investment cycle has over-indexed on application-layer and foundation model plays, creating a relative scarcity of deals in the infrastructure layer. Daxe's combination of Graph RAG technology and confidential computing targets a specific gap in the federal sector market that large AI players are structurally unable to serve in the near term. If they can convert the DoD relationship into a repeatable procurement mechanism, they become the standard-setter for an entirely underserved category. That's the bet. Everything else in the memo exists to pressure-test it.

Case Study Hopo Therapeutics — Impact Capital and the Right Investor Profile

Hopo is raising $3M in total, with $1.8M already committed from angel investors and impact VCs — a meaningful de-risking signal for the remaining $1.2M. The 94% gross margin on the manufactured compound (royalty included) is structurally unusual for biotech and reflects a manufacturing setup at LBNL that defers capital expenditure in the near term.

The Thesis question here is also an investor-fit question. Hopo's risk matrix summary score of 65.1/90 reflects a strong overall profile with specific, identifiable risks — all of them addressable with capital and time. An impact-oriented fund or a family office with a healthcare mandate and a long time horizon is the natural investor. A fund optimizing for 2-year DPI is not. The term sheet conversation should be explicit about this: the return thesis is a 4-year B2G contract milestone, and the right investor is one who views that as a feature, not a constraint.

The 5Ts aren't a checklist. They're a lens.

The goal of a framework isn't to reduce a company to a score. It's to make sure you're asking the question you're about to skip. Every deal memo I've written has a section I wanted to abbreviate — the one where the numbers are uncomfortable, or the risk is real, or the answer is "we don't know yet." The 5Ts, and their shadows, exist to make those sections unavoidable.

The best investment decisions I've seen don't resolve all uncertainty. They name it precisely. A good thesis is not "this company will succeed." It's "I believe this specific thing about the future, and if I'm right, this company is worth backing."

The rest is execution — which, unfortunately, is not something any framework can guarantee.
